## OpenAI ChatGPT v1.2026.195 Sony.Vs.Udio.Lawsuit.md

**Q: Explain the Sony lawsuit against Udio.**

**A:** The lawsuit concerns alleged copyright infringement during **AI model training**, not merely the music that Udio generates. Sony alleges that Udio copied and ingested tens of thousands of Sony-owned sound recordings without permission to train its music-generation models. Udio disputes those allegations and has argued that its conduct is protected, at least in part, under copyright doctrines such as fair use. ([The Verge][1])

### Background

In June 2024, the major record labels, including Sony Music Entertainment, Universal Music Group, and Warner Records, filed lawsuits against Udio and Suno. The complaints alleged that the companies:

* Copied copyrighted sound recordings on a massive scale.
* Used those recordings to train generative AI models without obtaining licenses.
* Built commercial products from that training.

The labels characterized this as systematic copyright infringement rather than incidental or transformative use. ([RIAA][2])

### Why Sony filed a second lawsuit

During discovery in the original case, Sony says it obtained information about Udio's training data. Using audio fingerprinting techniques, Sony alleges it identified **more than 30,000 additional recordings** that were used without authorization.

Sony attempted to add those recordings to the existing lawsuit. The federal judge declined to expand the original case because doing so late in discovery would dramatically increase its scope and complexity. The ruling was procedural rather than a determination on whether infringement occurred. ([Music Business Worldwide][3])

Instead, Sony filed a **new, separate lawsuit** covering approximately **30,117 recordings**. According to Sony, those works represent only part of the allegedly infringed catalog. ([The Verge][1])

### Sony's principal allegations

Sony alleges that Udio:

* Copied protected sound recordings without authorization.
* Used those recordings to train commercial AI models.
* Benefited commercially from copyrighted works while avoiding licensing fees.
* Continues to infringe Sony's copyrights.

Sony seeks:

* An injunction preventing further unauthorized use of its recordings.
* Statutory damages of up to **$150,000 per willfully infringed work**, if willful infringement is ultimately proven. Actual damages, if awarded instead, could differ substantially. ([The Verge][1])

### Udio's position

Udio has not conceded liability. Among its principal arguments are:

* Generative AI training differs from simply redistributing copyrighted recordings.
* Its models learn statistical relationships rather than storing songs for playback.
* Certain aspects of model training may qualify as fair use under U.S. copyright law.

The exact scope of those defenses has not yet been resolved by the courts. ([RIAA][2])

### Why Universal and Warner are no longer in the case

An important development is that **Universal Music Group** and **Warner Music** reached settlements and licensing agreements with Udio. Those companies are now collaborating with Udio on licensed AI music initiatives.

Sony chose not to settle and remains the principal major-label plaintiff actively litigating against Udio. ([The Verge][1])

### Why this case matters

The lawsuit is one of the most significant tests of AI copyright law because it addresses a fundamental question:

> **Does training a generative AI model on copyrighted creative works without a license constitute copyright infringement, or can it qualify as fair use?**

The answer will affect not only AI music systems such as Udio and Suno, but potentially other generative AI systems trained on copyrighted media, including images, video, books, and software. No definitive appellate precedent has yet resolved this issue across generative AI training generally, so the outcome could influence future licensing practices and AI development. ([RIAA][2])

**Q: If the model is being trained to say emulate the music style of the artist Prince, does not constitute fair-use; training the model to construct a contemporary Phrygian chord does constitute fair-use, in my opinion.**

**A:** That distinction is close to one of the central legal and technical questions in these cases.

There is a meaningful difference between learning **general musical principles** and learning **the expressive content of specific copyrighted works**.

| Training objective                                                                                                | Copyright implications                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Music theory (Phrygian mode, chord construction, counterpoint, voice leading, rhythm, orchestration)              | Generally favors fair use because these are ideas, systems, and techniques, which copyright does not protect.                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| Learning generic stylistic traits common to a genre (e.g., funk basslines, jazz harmony, Baroque fugue structure) | More likely to be permissible, although the line is not sharply defined. Copyright protects expression, not genres.                                                                                                                                                                               |
| Learning the recognizable style of a living or recently deceased artist (e.g., "compose like Prince")             | Legally unsettled. Style itself is generally not protected by copyright, but outputs that are substantially similar to copyrighted works can create infringement issues. Other legal theories, such as publicity or unfair competition, may also arise depending on the jurisdiction and context. |
| Memorizing and reproducing protected recordings or compositions                                                   | Least likely to qualify as fair use if substantial protected expression is copied.                                                                                                                                                                                                                |

The distinction you propose resembles a principle often discussed in copyright scholarship:

* **Abstract musical knowledge** (scales, modes, harmony, rhythm, instrumentation, production techniques) is analogous to learning grammar or mathematics.
* **Specific artistic expression** (melodies, lyrics, arrangements, performances, recordings) is what copyright is designed to protect.

For example, a model that learns:

* how a Phrygian cadence functions,
* how syncopation creates groove,
* how sevenths resolve in jazz harmony,

is learning concepts that musicians routinely study from textbooks and recordings.

By contrast, if the model effectively internalizes the specific melodic vocabulary, harmonic progressions, production choices, and signature motifs that make a Prince recording recognizable, a court may conclude that the training involved copying protected expression rather than merely extracting unprotectable ideas.

One complication is that neural networks do not explicitly separate "theory" from "expression." During optimization, they learn statistical relationships across the training corpus. A model has no internal notion of "this is music theory" versus "this is Prince's copyrighted melody." That makes it difficult to demonstrate exactly what information the model retained from any particular recording.

This is one reason the current litigation focuses less on what the model *learned* and more on **whether making copies of copyrighted recordings for training required authorization in the first place**. The courts may never need to decide whether the model learned only theory or memorized expression if they determine that the intermediate copying itself was or was not lawful.

Your distinction nevertheless aligns with a longstanding principle in copyright law: **ideas, methods, systems, and facts are not protected; the original expression of those ideas is.** The challenge in AI cases is determining where learned statistical representations fall along that spectrum.
