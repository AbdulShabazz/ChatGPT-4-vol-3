## The dilemma

**Dangerous counterfeit or substandard airbag inflators are already installed in an unknown number of U.S. used vehicles, but the government has no practical way to identify those vehicles or conduct a conventional recall.**

The parts typically enter the market after an original airbag deploys or is stolen. A repairer, rebuilder, or reseller installs a cheap replacement—sometimes bearing counterfeit automaker markings—and the vehicle later returns to the used-car market without the buyer knowing. Some inflators rupture during otherwise survivable crashes, projecting metal fragments into occupants rather than cushioning them. NHTSA currently attributes **10 deaths and three severe injuries in 13 crashes** to inflators marked **DTN60DB**. ([NHTSA][1])

### Why it is unusually difficult

| Problem                                            | Consequence                                                                                                                     |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **No Vehicle Identification Number (VIN) linkage** | The inflator was installed after manufacture, so a normal recall/VIN lookup cannot identify affected cars.                      |
| **Unknown supply chain**                           | Parts were likely imported illegally and distributed through repair shops, rebuilders, online marketplaces, and intermediaries. |
| **Unknown population**                             | Regulators do not know how many entered the country or which makes and models contain them.                                     |
| **Hidden inside the steering wheel**               | Owners generally cannot identify one visually without professional disassembly, which is itself hazardous.                      |
| **Incomplete vehicle histories**                   | Crash, theft, airbag-deployment, and repair records may be absent—particularly on rebuilt or informally repaired vehicles.      |
| **No responsible manufacturer/dealer network**     | There may be no traceable company capable of notifying owners and paying for repairs.                                           |
| **Owner bears the immediate cost**                 | NHTSA says owners generally must pay for inspection and replacement themselves.                                                 |

NHTSA banned the importation and sale of the identified defective inflators on **April 29, 2026**, but the ban mainly stops future commerce; it does not reveal where previously installed parts are located. NHTSA explicitly states that a traditional recall is unlikely because no affected-vehicle list exists. ([NHTSA][2])

Although the known ruptures have involved Chevrolet Malibu and Hyundai Sonata vehicles, regulators say there is insufficient evidence to conclude that the risk is limited to those models. The broader warning applies primarily to vehicles whose airbags deployed or were replaced—especially salvage/rebuilt vehicles and non-dealer repairs performed since 2020. ([NHTSA][3])

**In essence:** the United States can outlaw the part, intercept shipments, and prosecute sellers, but it cannot recall what it cannot trace.
