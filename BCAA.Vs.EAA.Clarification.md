<b>Q: Xtend bcaa powder is much too sweet tasting and it has citric acid flavor which is not compatible with most drinks I can compose on my palate. It also has like 6 aminos. Aren't 13 essential BCAAs?</b>

**A:** No. There are **9 essential amino acids (EAAs)** in adults, and only **3 of those are branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs)**.

| Category       | Amino acids                                                         |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **BCAAs**      | Leucine, isoleucine, valine                                         |
| **Other EAAs** | Lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, histidine |
| **Total EAAs** | **9**                                                               |

The confusion with **13** may come from products that combine essential amino acids with several **conditionally essential** or nonessential amino acids.

Regarding Xtend: the classic Xtend BCAA formulation is fundamentally a **3-BCAA product**, typically using a 2:1:1 leucine:isoleucine:valine ratio. Other amino-acid-like ingredients may be included depending on the specific Xtend version, but they do not turn it into a complete EAA supplement.

### Your taste objection points toward a different product category

If you dislike the **strong sweetener + citric/malic acid sports-drink profile**, I would not look for another flavored BCAA powder. Look for either:

1. **Unflavored EAA powder** containing all 9 EAAs. This is nutritionally more comprehensive than BCAAs alone.
2. **Unflavored BCAA powder** if you specifically want only leucine, isoleucine and valine. Be aware that raw BCAAs, particularly leucine, can be distinctly bitter and poorly soluble.
3. **EAA capsules/tablets** if taste is the overriding problem.

For muscle protein synthesis, **EAAs are generally more rational than isolated BCAAs** because BCAAs can stimulate the signaling machinery, particularly through leucine, but the body still needs the other essential amino acids as substrates to construct complete proteins.

**Breadcrumbs:** 20 proteinogenic amino acids → 9 essential amino acids → 3 BCAAs → leucine/isoleucine/valine.

<b>Q: BCAAs can "stimulate the signaling machinery"?</b>

**A:** Yes. More precisely, **leucine acts partly as a nutrient signal**, not merely as a building block for protein.

When blood and intracellular leucine rise after eating protein or taking Branched-Chain Amino Acids (BCAAs), leucine helps activate the **mTORC1 pathway**. Mechanistically, leucine sensing involves proteins including **Sestrin2** and the **Rag GTPase** machinery, which promote mTORC1 activation.

The simplified sequence is:

**Leucine rises → leucine sensing → mTORC1 activation → translation machinery increases activity → muscle protein synthesis is stimulated**

This produces an important distinction:

* **Leucine:** both a protein substrate and a strong anabolic nutrient signal.
* **Isoleucine and valine:** substrates with metabolic roles, but substantially weaker mTORC1 signaling effects.
* **All 9 Essential Amino Acids (EAAs):** provide the complete essential substrate set required to sustain new protein synthesis.

An analogy is that **leucine presses the "start" button on a protein-assembly line**, but pressing start does not supply all the parts. If the other EAAs are unavailable, muscle protein synthesis cannot remain elevated simply because large amounts of leucine or BCAAs are present.

This is one reason isolated BCAA supplementation has a somewhat peculiar nutritional proposition. If someone already consumes adequate high-quality protein, they are generally already receiving substantial BCAAs along with the other EAAs needed to use them.

**mTORC1 → translation initiation → muscle protein synthesis; leucine = signal + substrate; EAAs = complete essential substrate set.**

### Why unflavored amino acids taste bad

**Leucine is bitter and relatively poorly soluble in water.** Isoleucine and valine also contribute bitterness. A raw 2:1:1 BCAA mixture therefore does not taste neutral.

A complete EAA mixture introduces additional taste problems. In particular, **phenylalanine, methionine and tryptophan** can contribute unpleasant bitter or sulfurous notes. Consequently, an unflavored EAA powder can taste considerably more medicinal than something like unflavored creatine.

This explains the formulation you dislike in Xtend. Manufacturers commonly attack amino-acid bitterness with:

**high-intensity sweetener + citric/malic acid + strong fruit flavor → masking of amino-acid bitterness**

The citric acid is therefore doing more than making it taste like a sports drink. **Acidity is part of the flavor-masking system.**

### For your use case

If the objective is an amino-acid powder that can disappear into coffee, milk, juice, smoothies, flavored water, etc., neither raw BCAA nor raw EAA powder is ideal.

There is an interesting alternative: **use a neutral protein source instead of free-form amino acids**. Whey protein naturally supplies all nine EAAs, including substantial leucine, but its amino acids are incorporated into peptides/proteins rather than present predominantly as free amino acids. That changes the sensory characteristics dramatically.

For example, roughly **20–30 g of a high-quality whey protein** can provide approximately **2–3 g leucine plus the other EAAs**, while avoiding the extraordinary bitterness of putting equivalent quantities of free leucine/EAA powder into a drink.

If you specifically want **low-calorie, nearly flavor-neutral, free-form amino acids**, I can look for current unflavored EAA/BCAA formulations with minimal or no citric acid, sucralose, acesulfame-K, or flavoring.

<b>Q: Does hot coffee breakdown the free form acids?</b>

**A:** No. **Normal hot coffee will not meaningfully break down free-form essential amino acids (EAAs).** You can mix an unflavored EAA powder into coffee.

Coffee is typically consumed around **55–85 °C (131–185 °F)**. At these temperatures and over the few minutes the amino acids remain in the drink, the EAAs are generally chemically stable enough for nutritional purposes.

There are a few nuances:

* **Heat alone:** Normal coffee temperatures are far below conditions required for rapid thermal decomposition of most amino acids.
* **Coffee acidity:** Coffee's mildly acidic pH does not destroy EAAs during normal preparation/consumption times.
* **Maillard reactions:** Amino acids can react with reducing sugars under sufficiently strong heating, but this becomes much more important during prolonged cooking/roasting than after adding EAAs to brewed coffee.
* **Tryptophan and methionine:** These are among the more chemically sensitive EAAs, but a few minutes in hot coffee should not produce nutritionally significant losses.
* **Do not brew the powder with the coffee grounds.** Add it **after brewing**. That avoids exposing it to the highest temperatures and unnecessary heating time.

For minimizing both degradation and taste impact, an effective procedure is:

**Brew coffee → allow it to cool briefly to roughly 60–70 °C (140–158 °F) → add EAA powder → stir thoroughly → add milk/cream as desired.**

The larger problem is likely **taste, not stability**. Coffee's existing bitterness may actually integrate the bitterness of free leucine, isoleucine, valine, phenylalanine, etc., better than water does.

**Keywords:** free-form EAAs → thermally stable at beverage temperatures → short exposure → negligible nutritional degradation → add after brewing.

<b>Q: Is the lead warning, per dose or per product contents?</b>

**A:** **Per exposure/daily consumption, not per entire container.**

For California Proposition 65, the relevant threshold is based on the **amount of lead a person is exposed to**, generally evaluated as a daily exposure under the product's reasonably anticipated use. It is not saying that the whole tub contains more than 0.5 µg of lead. ([OEHHA][1])

For lead, California's established Maximum Allowable Dose Level (MADL) for reproductive toxicity is **0.5 µg/day**. ([OEHHA][2])

So, for Nutricost EAA:

* Container: **30 servings**.
* Recommended use presumably establishes the expected daily exposure.
* The warning means Nutricost believes or assumes consumption can produce a Proposition-65-relevant lead exposure; **the warning itself does not disclose the actual µg of lead per scoop**. ([Nutricost][3])
* It therefore **does not mean 0.5 µg is distributed throughout the entire 30-serving container**.

There is another important qualification: a Prop 65 warning **does not prove that Nutricost measured >0.5 µg per serving**. California allows businesses to issue warnings based on knowledge or assumptions about exposure without actually establishing the precise concentration through testing. ([OEHHA][4])

So we still need the **batch Certificate of Analysis (CoA)** to answer the question you probably care about: *How many micrograms of lead are actually in one scoop of Blue Raspberry EAA?*

<b>Q: However if someone does receive lead poisoning, they have no legal recourse?</b>

**A:** No. **A Proposition 65 warning does not generally eliminate a consumer's legal recourse if the product actually causes an injury.** It primarily addresses California's statutory *duty to warn* about exposure to listed chemicals. OEHHA describes the “safe harbor” as protection from **Proposition 65 warning requirements** when applicable conditions are satisfied, not blanket immunity from product-liability law. ([OEHHA][1])

If a consumer could establish actual lead poisoning caused by the supplement, other theories could potentially remain available, such as defective-product liability, negligence, breach of warranty, misrepresentation, or an inadequate-warning claim. The precise claims depend heavily on the jurisdiction and facts. A warning could nevertheless be important evidence for the manufacturer: it may make a *failure-to-warn* theory more difficult if the warning adequately disclosed the relevant risk. It does not necessarily resolve whether the product was defectively contaminated or otherwise unreasonably dangerous.

There is also an important evidentiary issue. A claimant would generally need to establish **causation**: not merely elevated blood lead, but evidence connecting the injury to that particular product. California product-liability law treats causation as a substantive requirement; an adequate or inadequate warning does not dispense with it. ([Supreme Court of California][2])

So the warning should not be interpreted as:

> “This contains lead; by consuming it you waive liability.”

Its legal function is closer to:

> “This product may expose you to a listed chemical; we are providing the California-required exposure warning.”

OEHHA itself emphasizes that Proposition 65 regulates **exposure and warnings**, rather than simply setting a permissible concentration of a chemical in the product. ([OEHHA][3])

For a supplement intended for **routine daily consumption**, I would therefore put considerably more weight on an actual batch heavy-metal Certificate of Analysis than on the presence or absence of a Prop 65 warning.
